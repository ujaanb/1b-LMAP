What is the role of coaching?

What is the role of health coaching?

Coaching can facilitate individual-level behaviour change and patient-centred care.

Globally, what percentage of deaths do non-communicable diseases account for?

75% (e.g. cardiovascular disease, stroke, cancer, chronic lung disease, and diabetes).

Globally, what percentage of heart disease and stroke are preventable?

80%.

Globally, what percentage of cancer deaths are preventable?

30–50%.

Person-centred care and coaching

What is health coaching?

  • The art of facilitating the patient’s active participation in managing their own health, inspired by the person-centred approach.
  • The person-centred approach allows us to shift from asking “What is the matter with you?” → “What matters to you?”

Describe the importance of person-centred approaches.

Person-centred approaches including health coaching are increasingly recognised to be at the heart of shared decision-making and support patients to actively participate in managing their health and wellbeing through being co-partners in the healthcare journey.

Principles of health coaching

What is health coaching? (process definition)

  • The process of supporting someone to think about their health goals and working with them to identify an action plan.
  • Health coaching draws upon: motivational interviewing (MI), psychological therapies, the positive psychology movement, sports coaching and executive coaching.

What are the six principles of health coaching?

  1. Patients are resourceful
  2. The practitioner’s role is to move from expert to enabler
  3. A whole-life approach is important as the patient’s health will be affected by everything else going on in their life
  4. The patient brings the agenda to the consultation
  5. The practitioner and patient are equals in the consultation
  6. Coaching is about change and action

Describe how important the patient being resourceful is.

  • Can the patient retain and understand information and weigh up pros and cons, taking responsibility for themselves and their choices?
  • Someone with end-stage dementia would not be able to do this, but someone with a new diagnosis of dementia at a very early stage most likely would.
  • Someone intoxicated with drugs or alcohol would not; someone with substance misuse who has insight and is not intoxicated may respond positively to a coaching approach.

Describe how important trust is in the health coaching relationship.

A coaching approach will not be appropriate if you believe the patient is being manipulative and dishonest, or if they believe the same about you and do not trust or respect you.

Describe how important change and action is.

  • Coaching is about change and action.
  • The patient needs to at some level want to address their issue and be prepared to explore this further. If not, a health coaching conversation may feel stuck and even be counter-productive.
  • In this situation, approach non-judgementally, offer brief health information if appropriate, and let the patient know they can discuss the issue again when ready.

Describe the two main ways that health coaching may be implemented.

  • Referral: patients may be referred to a health coach for support on health behaviour change (diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol).
  • Opportunistic: used within routine consultations — asking one or two key coaching questions can help a patient consider new perspectives.

Describe who else we may involve in the health coaching process.

Coaching approaches can also be helpful during conversations with patients’ families (parents/guardians, carers) to explore perspectives and values and enable shared decision-making.

Describe how we can decide whether health coaching may be applied.

  • There may be situations where coaching approaches are not appropriate.
  • Rather than thinking about whether specific conditions or types of patients exclude coaching, it is more helpful to consider whether the coaching principles can apply.

Core skills in health coaching conversations

Describe how expectations can be clarified within a health coaching relationship.

  • Practicalities: agree location and timing of consultations.
  • Approach to confidentiality: discuss from the outset, including that confidentiality may need to be broken if there are concerns about risk of harm to others or the patient.
  • Expectations of roles: being clear from the start reduces misunderstandings. In health coaching the practitioner shifts from expert to enabler, helping the patient set their own goals and identify their own solutions.
  • Boundaries: clarify limits of your competence and who else you may need to signpost to.
  • Keeping a record: a written record of what has been discussed and agreed helps you both keep track.

Describe what rapport entails.

  • Full attention, mutual respect, empathy and a curious, non-judgemental mindset.
  • Where there are problems with the relationship dynamic, reflect on the mindset that practitioner and patient are bringing — awareness helps identify what needs to change.

Describe the importance of rapport.

  • Rapport provides the foundation for effective healthcare interactions.
  • Building rapport helps participants feel they connect with each other through developing a better understanding of each other’s thoughts and feelings, leading to better communication.

Describe how we may potentially identify problems with rapport.

  • Alignment of body language and tone of voice can indicate a higher level of rapport.
  • Notice whether this alignment is present or absent and take steps to rebuild rapport if needed.

Describe three ways rapport can be developed.

  • Be fully present in the conversation so you can actively listen.
  • Active listening enables non-verbal skills more naturally (eye contact, nodding, leaning forward).
  • If writing notes, maintain rapport through non-verbal cues.
  • Think about how to maximise rapport in remote contexts (telephone/video).

Describe three ways rapport can be developed in remote conversations.

  • If meeting for the first time, spend more time with introductions and rapport-building.
  • Speak slower with clear language and more pauses, including where the internet connection is slower.
  • For video calls, maximise capturing non-verbal communication by including neck, upper body and arms as well as head; encourage the patient to do the same.
  • Tone of voice becomes even more important when there are no visual cues.
  • Looking at the camera vs the patient’s face on screen — you may find looking at the face more natural; try moving the screen image closer to the camera. Rapport involves more than eye contact.
  • Use a shared whiteboard if available so both can contribute to planning next steps.
  • Use plain, uncluttered backgrounds for video calls, with light directly on your face — especially important with lower-quality internet.

Describe the type of questions that need to be asked in health coaching conversations.

  • Ask solution-oriented questions that help generate new thinking and move a person forward.
  • NOTE: The way we phrase questions directly determines the responses we get.

Describe the importance of tone in health coaching conversations.

  • A judgemental tone can discount all the benefits of a well-worded question.
  • A non-judgemental tone can enable you to ask powerful, solution-oriented questions.
