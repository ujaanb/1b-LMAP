An introduction to motivational interviewing

What is motivational interviewing?

  • A collaborative, person-centred form of guiding to elicit and strengthen motivation to change.
  • Developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick; originated from work in the 1970s–80s with substance misuse; now used more widely in promoting health behaviour change.

Describe components that make up the “spirit” of motivational interviewing.

  • Compassion: act from a desire to support the patient’s wellbeing
  • Acceptance: respectful, non-judgemental approach; value and affirm the patient’s autonomy and worth
  • Partnership: work in partnership; patient is resourceful and an ‘expert’ in their own situation
  • Evocation: help the patient identify their own perspectives and motivation for change

Describe the difference between motivational interviewing and health coaching.

  • In health coaching, the patient sets the agenda for the conversation, which can focus on a variety of problems from social issues to treatment options.
  • In motivational interviewing, the practitioner creates the agenda, which focuses on changing behaviour in a well-defined way.

OARS — motivational interviewing skills

What is the OARS model?

A core communication technique used in motivational interviewing (MI).

Describe the components of the OARS model.

  • Open questions
  • Affirmations
  • Reflective listening
  • Summarising

Describe the importance of open questions.

  • Help explore meaning, values and goals, and the patient’s thoughts and feelings about their issue.
  • Invite more than a simple yes/no answer.
  • Reduce the possibility of ‘advice in disguise’ (e.g. Have you thought about…?, Do you think…?, Is this a good idea…?).
  • Advice in disguise is often phrased as closed questions — can be disempowering and may result in barriers.
  • Coaching is not about providing a solution you think is best, but supporting them to find their own.

Describe how affirmations can be used.

  • Positive statements or gestures that emphasise the patient’s strengths and abilities.
  • Help acknowledge behaviours directed towards positive change, no matter how big or small.
  • Help build self-efficacy and increase motivation and confidence.
  • Must be expressed genuinely.

Describe how reflective listening can be used.

  • Actively listening enables you to pick up on and reflect back the patient’s own words or behaviours.
  • Particularly useful where the patient has repeated a word/phrase, or used unusual or particularly emotive language.
  • As well as building rapport and empathy, describing what you have noticed can prompt further exploration of barriers and enablers, and develop new insights.
  • Examples: “You used the word ‘busy’ a few times. Can you say more about that?” / “I noticed you smiled when you said that.”

What is “change talk”?

  • The positive and hopeful language that a patient uses when talking about making a health behaviour change.
  • You can help build confidence and motivation by reflecting back this language and their reason for change.

Describe how summarising can be used.

  • Helps check that you have understood correctly and gives the patient an opportunity to correct you.
  • Useful when there are signs of resistance that may signify the patient does not feel ready for change.
  • Conveys that they have been listened to and understood; can reflect ambivalence and highlight discrepancies with their desired goal.
  • Selectively summarising the patient’s ‘change talk’ can strengthen motivation.

The four processes of MI

What are the four processes of MI?

  • Engaging: building connection and rapport with patients.
  • Focusing: helping patients identify a health behaviour-related goal to focus on during the coaching interaction.
  • Evoking: supporting patients to explore their situation further and evoke thoughts and feelings about their goal — including why it is important and what barriers are in the way; reinforce ‘change talk’.
  • Planning: supporting patients to identify options, opportunities and resources, address what might get in the way, and plan next steps for action.

What is focusing?

Helping patients to identify a health behaviour-related goal that they can then focus on during the coaching interaction.

Example focusing questions.

  • What is the issue?
  • What makes this an issue now?
  • What is important to you about this issue?
  • How motivated are you to address this issue?
  • What part will be most useful to focus on in the next x minutes?
  • What do you want ideally? Often people come with an issue which they convert into what they think is a goal.

Goal-setting: common problems and alternatives.

  • “I want to lead a healthy lifestyle” — too vague. Ask: What does ‘healthy’ mean for you? What would it look/feel like if you achieved this? What would other people notice?
  • “I don’t want to eat junk food any more” — phrased as moving away from something. Help the patient rephrase into something they can move towards (a positive, approachable goal).
  • “I want my partner to cook healthier food for me” — externalises the goal onto someone else; help the patient identify what is within their own control and what they want ideally for themselves.

What is evoking?

Supporting patients to explore their situation further and evoke their thoughts and feelings about their goal — including why the goal is important and what barriers are in their way.

Describe the role that evoking plays in motivational interviewing.

Having agreed a goal or direction for the conversation, the next stage is to help the patient explore their current situation further and support them to generate new insights.
