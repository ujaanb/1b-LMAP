MECC and Very Brief Advice (VBA) in smoking

What is Making Every Contact Count (MECC)?

An approach to behaviour change that aims to give everyone in an organisation the tools and confidence to promote positive health messages to everyone they encounter.

What is it that makes smoking addictive?

  • Most smokers are actually addicted to nicotine.
  • Inhaled nicotine from tobacco smoke is as or more addictive than heroin or cocaine.

What is the problem with advising patients to “stop smoking”?

  • It is a negative message
  • It comes across as nagging
  • It is nothing new to the smoker — they’ve heard it all before
  • Encourages conflict and denial
  • Frustrating for both doctor and smoker
  • Takes longer
  • Puts you off asking about smoking again

Why have a lot of smokers not stopped smoking?

  • 70% of smokers want to stop smoking and 83% wish they’d never started.
  • The problem is that they don’t know how to stop.

Describe the three key elements to successfully stopping smoking.

  • Wanting to stop smoking — but for 95–97% of smokers, willpower is not enough
  • Good quality support
  • Evidence-based treatments — varenicline, nicotine replacement therapies, bupropion

What is ‘Very Brief Advice’ (VBA)?

A simple form of advice designed for busy clinicians to be used opportunistically in less than 30 seconds in almost any consultation with a smoker — telling them how to stop and directing them appropriately.

Recall the three elements of VBA.

  • Ask
  • Advise
  • Act

Describe what each of the three elements of VBA entails.

  • ASK: establish and record smoking status — ‘Do you smoke? Are you still smoking?’ If known to have smoked in the past, re-check status.
  • ADVISE: on how to stop — ‘The best way to stop is with support and treatment’.
  • ACT: offer support and treatment — ‘When you are ready just make an appointment with (name) who is great!’

What is the one thing that VBA attempts not to do?

  • VBA deliberately avoids ‘challenging the addiction’ so does not: advise smokers to stop; ask how much or what they smoke; ask if they want to stop.
  • Leaving these questions out takes practise and can be the most difficult part, especially for experienced clinicians.

Describe what the “Advise” part of VBA entails.

A simple statement advising that the best way to stop is with a combination of support and treatment, which can increase the chance of stopping many times.

Describe what to do if a smoker doesn’t feel ready to stop after hearing advice.

  • Don’t push them to stop
  • Make it clear the door is always open when they’re ready

Describe the benefits of VBA.

  • Brief! (<30 seconds or it won’t be used)
  • Records smoking status (as 70% relapse rate)
  • Positive (otherwise you put them off trying)
  • Not confrontational or nagging (not telling them to stop)
  • Opportunistic (suitable for almost any consultation)
  • Informative (saying how to stop)
  • Engaging (new information)
  • Evidence based
  • Not a smoking cessation consult (that’s for next time)
  • In time-consuming scenarios, it is reasonable to ask to make an appointment for a dedicated consultation about smoking cessation

SUMMARY: Very Brief Advice (VBA).

  • Brief advice to smokers is one of the most cost-effective of all healthcare interventions
  • VBA can be given in almost any consultation
  • Advising smokers to stop is not effective
  • VBA avoids ‘challenging the addiction’
  • Always advise support and medication
  • Willpower alone is seldom effective
  • REMEMBER: Most smokers know they should stop. What they don’t know is how.

Smoking cessation

Describe the global health burden of smoking.

  • Smoking is the largest preventable cause of disease and premature death in the world.
  • More than 50% of long-term smokers die prematurely due to smoking-related diseases.

What’s in a cigarette?

Cigarette smoke contains more than 4,000 chemicals, including over 60 known carcinogens and metabolic poisons.

Describe why smoking is so dangerous to so many organs.

  • The route of delivery (lungs) is designed for maximum ill effect — smoke is quickly absorbed, unfiltered into the bloodstream and spreads to the whole body in seconds.
  • This is why there is no organ spared damage from smoking.

Describe three reasons why people continue to smoke despite the adverse effects.

  • Habit
  • Boredom
  • Stress
  • Social
  • Taste
  • Weight control

What is the main reason why people continue to smoke despite the adverse effects?

Above all, it is nicotine addiction that causes people to continue to smoke.

Describe how addictive inhaled nicotine is.

Inhaled nicotine from tobacco smoke is as or more addictive than heroin or cocaine.

Describe how dangerous nicotine is.

  • Out of all the chemicals in cigarette smoke, nicotine is relatively harmless.
  • It’s the nicotine which makes you smoke, but it’s the smoke which kills you.

Describe the effects of nicotine within the brain.

  • Nicotine has the highest binding affinity of the alpha4beta2 (α4β2) nicotinic receptors, the main mediators of nicotine addiction in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) of the midbrain.
  • Inhaled nicotine reaches these receptors in just 7 to 10 seconds, rapidly triggering release of dopamine.

Describe the long-term effects of nicotine within the brain.

  • Regular smoking increases the number of brain α4β2 receptors by three or four times, which enhances the addictive effect of inhaled nicotine.
  • These receptor changes are long term, which makes it easy for smokers to go back to smoking even years after they’ve stopped.

Is smoking a disease or a lifestyle choice?

REMEMBER: For most smokers, smoking is a chronic relapsing, organic brain disease, not simply a lifestyle choice.

Three key elements to successfully stopping (revisited) + note.

  • Wanting to stop (willpower alone not enough for 95–97%)
  • Good quality support
  • Evidence-based treatments (varenicline, NRT, bupropion)
  • NOTE: Good quality support and evidence-based treatments are not as effective on their own, but together they can increase cessation rates by up to four times.

Describe the goals of behavioural support to stop smoking.

  • Reduce motivation to smoke
  • Bolster commitment to abstinence
  • Enhance ability to cope with cravings
  • Ensure effective use of pharmacotherapy

Describe two skills required to provide effective behavioural support.

  • An understanding of the essentials of evidence-based smoking cessation
  • Good communication skills

Describe how the current NHS Stop Smoking Services work.

  • Traditionally delivered by NHS Stop Smoking Services via trained smoking cessation specialists.
  • Standard support regime: 4–6 face-to-face appointments over 6–12 weeks.
  • May be administered by: practice nurses, hospital nurses, HCAs, pharmacists, pharmacy assistants, trained specialist stop smoking advisors.

Describe the advantages of a doctor delivering smoking cessation advice.

  • Tobacco dependence is a medical condition that responds to medical treatment
  • Doctors are independent prescribers
  • Cessation advice can be more effective from doctors than counsellors and nurses
  • Smokers know, trust and want help to stop from their doctor

Describe the three licensed pharmacotherapies for smoking cessation in the UK.

  • Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT)
  • Bupropion
  • Varenicline
  • NOTE: They all work differently and have slightly different success rates. Varenicline has been shown to be the most effective, closely followed by a combination of two or more forms of NRT, then bupropion. All three should be considered with any smoker thinking of stopping.

Describe three reasons why it is important to consider all options available.

  • What works well for one person may not be so good for another
  • It allows the patient to choose the treatment they want — self-determination is important in behavioural change
  • If a quit attempt fails (i.e. relapse), the smoker knows there is an alternative option

Recall the eight forms that NRT is available in.

  • Skin patches
  • Gum
  • Lozenges
  • Mini lozenges
  • Inhalator
  • Oral spray
  • Nasal spray
  • Oral film

Describe the principle behind NRT.

  • Replace nicotine from cigarettes with so-called therapeutic nicotine, which has none of the harmful ingredients of cigarette smoke.
  • After a few months, the aim is to stop the nicotine altogether.

Describe how bupropion works.

  • A non-nicotine prescription tablet originally developed to treat depression.
  • Maintains higher levels of dopamine and noradrenergic activity.
  • This reduces the cravings and withdrawal symptoms, allowing people to stop smoking more easily.

Describe how varenicline works.

  • Partially stimulating and also partially blocking the α4β2 nicotinic receptors.
  • Stimulation gives a limited delivery of dopamine which relieves cravings and withdrawal symptoms, while blockade causes reduced reward from smoking.

Describe the advantages of e-cigarettes.

  • Over 3 million users in the UK
  • 95% safer than smoking
  • At least as effective as NRT for smoking cessation
  • Great potential for smoking cessation

Describe the disadvantages of e-cigarettes.

  • Long-term safety not known
  • Half of e-cigarette users also smoke
  • Controversial and many misconceptions

Describe the overall effects of support and medication on smoking cessation.

  • 20–30% long-term quit rate at each quit attempt — so prepare for a high relapse rate; still really good vs only 3–5% with willpower alone.
  • It takes two or three supported attempts for >50% of smokers to stop long term — if smokers don’t stop, more than 50% die years early, often after decades of illness.
  • These smoking cessation interventions with treatment and support are hence some of the most effective in healthcare.

Brief interventions in alcohol use (IBA)

What is Identification and Brief Advice (IBA)?

A simple, structured approach used primarily in healthcare and community settings to identify risky behaviours (especially alcohol misuse), offer quick, non-judgmental support, and encourage positive change.

Describe why it is important to address alcohol misuse in the UK.

  • The vast majority of British adults still drink alcohol — this takes its toll on the nation’s health.
  • 1.6 million people may have some level of alcohol dependence; in 2017 there were nearly 21,000 admissions with alcoholic liver disease (57% rise from 2005).
  • Reducing alcohol use is one of the most effective interventions for improving our health and should be a priority for clinicians.

Describe the three components of IBA.

  • Identification: who’s at risk from alcohol use?
  • Brief Advice: advice on how alcohol use can be cut down
  • Support: self-help or referral to a specialised alcohol service

Describe the advantage of IBA.

Can be delivered in a matter of minutes — perfect for GP consultations or when working on the wards.

Describe how Identification is performed in IBA.

  • It can be difficult to objectively understand how much someone consumes, therefore we use screening tools.
  • In the UK, audit tools such as AUDIT-C are recommended.

Describe how AUDIT-C works.

  • Questions are asked and individuals are given a score depending on their answers.
  • NOTE: AUDIT-C is a shortened version of the screening audit tools used to identify those at risk of alcohol dependence, which can be used if you are short on time.

Describe how Brief Advice can be used in IBA.

  • Once you’ve identified somebody with risky drinking, the next step is brief advice.
  • Important that advice isn’t patronising or overbearing, as the patient might become defensive, leading to an unproductive conversation.
  • Instead, motivational interviewing skills should be used to prompt them to think about why reducing alcohol use might be beneficial.

Describe how implementation techniques can be applied to reduce alcohol consumption.

  • An implementation technique will allow individuals to state the practical steps they will take to achieve their goal.
  • It often details a specific what, where, when and how of change.

Describe three things that can increase success rates with Brief Advice.

  • Empathy
  • Non-judgmental
  • Self-efficacy
  • Facilitate the patient to make changes themselves

Describe how patients who are not suspected to be alcohol-dependent are managed.

  • Support (final step of IBA)
  • A range of self-help resources are available, including patient information resources, apps and alcohol consumption trackers
  • Patients can be directed to these resources

Describe how patients who are suspected to be alcohol-dependent are managed.

  • Support (final step of IBA)
  • For patients whose drinking habits indicate they may be dependent (i.e. AUDIT score >20), referral to specialised alcohol services is appropriate.
  • NOTE: Automatic referral by a clinician may lead to greater success than relying on a patient to refer themselves.

Brief interventions for physical activity

Describe the magnitude of the problem of physical inactivity.

Physical inactivity may be as harmful as cigarette smoking and obesity.

Describe the prevalence of physical inactivity.

  • In 2014: 1 in 4 women and 1 in 5 men were doing less than 30 minutes a week of physical activity.
  • In 2016: 1 in 3 men and 2 in 5 women were not active enough for good health.

Describe three factors that can increase one’s risk of being physically inactive.

  • Gender — women more likely to be inactive than men
  • Lower socio-economic status
  • Disability/long-term health condition
  • Ethnicity — some ethnic minorities

Describe one way we can address the problem of physical inactivity.

Stimulating people to reflect on or consider their own activity levels.

Describe the role of Brief Interventions in physical activity.

  • Although most people probably know that being active is beneficial, they may not necessarily acknowledge that their own levels of inactivity may be harmful.
  • Brief interventions can help start this process of developing insight prior to initiating change.
