Scientists, policy and the Overton Window

Describe the four types of scientist proposed by Pielke (2005).

  • Pure Scientist: focuses on research not its use; provides knowledge so decision makers can use it; no direct connection with decision makers
  • Issue Advocate: focuses on implications of research for a particular political agenda; aligns with a group advancing interests through policy/politics; engages with decision making; seeks to reduce scope of decision choice
  • Science Arbiter: stays removed from policy/politics; provides expert advice; only scientific input; does not interact with stakeholders
  • Honest Broker: integrates scientific knowledge with stakeholder concerns to identify/inform decision options; places science in context of options; seeks to expand scope of decision choice

What are policy entrepreneurs?

  • Kingdon (2003): “policy entrepreneurs” bring together problems, solutions, and politics to create change when windows of opportunity occur.
  • Can be chaotic, heavily influenced by people and events — sometimes years for change; sometimes days.

What is the Overton Window?

  • Explains how policy ideas shift from politically unthinkable to widely accepted.
  • Only ideas within the “window” are seen as politically viable — politicians can support them without risking their career.
  • Also: the range of mainstream and socially or politically acceptable ideas or policies at any given time.

Describe how the Overton window can be used to implement change.

  • Window can shift so once-marginal positions become mainstream.
  • Through: incremental policy changes leading public toward bigger shifts; focusing events/controversy/shock tactics that stretch discussion boundaries; persuasive framing and messaging campaigns that reshape how people perceive an issue.

Tame vs wicked problems

Describe the difference between tame and wicked problems.

  • Tame: can be simple or complex but solvable by linear process; we are typically trained for these; require a plan — e.g. managing a doctors’ rota.
  • Wicked: complex and complicated by multiple competing drivers, interests, uncertainty and competing solutions; many public policy and social protection (including healthcare) issues; require a strategy — e.g. reforming social care or improving urgent/emergency care in England.
  • Rittel and Webber (1973): wicked problems are complex, intractable, and difficult to solve.

Describe an example of a wicked problem.

  • Yearly A&E “winter crisis”.
  • Delivery plan objectives: (1) increase capacity (19 in 20 beds occupied); (2) grow the workforce; (3) speed up discharge; (4) expand community services (up to 20% emergency admissions avoidable); (5) help people access the right care first time (111 as first port of call).

How the NHS is run

Describe how the NHS is run on a national level.

  • Department of Health & Social Care: Wes Streeting (SoS DHSC); Professor Sir Chris Whitty (CMO).
  • NHS England: Amanda Pritchard (CEO NHSE); Professor Sir Stephen Powis (NMD).
  • NOTE: Hugely complex — over 100 different separate organisations and over 1.5 million employees.

Describe how the NHS is run on a local level.

  • Since 2022, every area in England covered by an ICS overseen by an ICB.
  • 42 Integrated Care Systems across the UK — designed to encourage partnership working between health and social care organisations in each local area.
  • They replace Clinical Commissioning Groups (CCGs).
  • Integrated Care Boards (ICBs): NHS-only management structures responsible for planning healthcare services for their local population.
