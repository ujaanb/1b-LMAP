Ethics and justice

What is ethics?

  • A set of social or personal values that affect how people make decisions and lead their lives.
  • Deals with questions of what’s right and what’s wrong, and what’s socially acceptable.

Describe the four pillars of medical ethics.

  • Autonomy (freedom to act independently)
  • Non-maleficence (do no harm)
  • Beneficence (do good)
  • Justice (fairness)
  • Created by Beauchamp and Childress, 1985

What is utilitarianism?

  • The ethically right choice in a given situation is the one that produces the most happiness and the least unhappiness for the largest number of people.
  • Utilitarianism is a branch of consequentialism; it contrasts with other ethical theories that morally evaluate actions on the basis of their intended consequences rather than outcomes.

What is consequentialism?

Argues that the ethical validity of an action is based on the outcome of that action — morality determined by consequences.

What is social justice?

The idea that everyone should have their fair chance in life — discussion evolving into what that fair chance might mean.

What is distributive justice?

  • Fair allocation of resources, opportunities, and burdens within a society to give everyone a fair chance.
  • John Rawls’ “Veil of Ignorance” thought experiment: imagine not knowing class/position/status you will be born into — describe the society you’d want; almost everyone chooses the fairer society with equality of opportunity.

Describe the impact of neglecting social justice.

“…neglect of the pursuit of social justice in all its dimensions translates into de facto acceptance of a future marred by violence, repression and chaos.”

Politics

What is politics?

  • Politics (big P): political parties and philosophy of government — driven by politicians; the activity of government.
  • Politics (little p): process of gaining or applying power in a local group or organisation.

What is the purpose of government?

  • Most basically: protect citizens from external threats; historically also to promote unity and fairness.
  • Good government about creating a better, fairer, juster society — accepted as necessary in most western democracies.

Describe basically the difference between left and right wing.

  • Left: more traditionally associated with challenging the status quo.
  • Right: traditionally more loyal to tradition and the current situation — hence liberal and conservative labels.

Describe the difference between libertarianism and collectivism.

  • Axis of how much the state should intervene.
  • Libertarianism (John Stuart Mill): state should not interfere in individual autonomy — only countenance interference if someone else is coming to harm.
  • Collectivist: more egalitarian state — equal opportunities; wealth redistributed partially by progressive taxation or wholly by state action on salaries/company profits; may be influenced by Marx or Lenin.

Describe the effect of adopting extreme sides of these political spectrums.

Most people fall in the middle — history shows taking ideologies to extremes almost always ends in more human suffering.

Describe how left and right wing ideologies are not enough to influence social policy.

For social policy (including health services/systems), think beyond simple left vs right — you will also have a view on how much the state should intervene in personal liberties.

Justification to intervene & effecting change

What is the Harm Principle?

  • “The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others.” — John Stuart Mill (1806–1873).
  • Forms the basis of Mill’s belief that individuals are sovereign and state interference should be constrained as the default.

What is a stakeholder?

An individual or organisation that has an interest in what you're doing.

What is a partner?

A specific subgroup of stakeholders who are central to the success of whatever it is you’re trying to achieve.

Describe three important things to know about stakeholders when trying to implement change.

  • Who are they?
  • Do they support you?
  • What can they do for you? (i.e. how powerful are they?)
