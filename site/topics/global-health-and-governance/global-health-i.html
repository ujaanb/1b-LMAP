Introduction to global health

What are the United Nations?

An international organisation founded in 1945 with the primary goal of maintaining international peace and security.

What is global health?

An area of study, research and practice that places a priority on improving health and achieving equity for all people worldwide.

Describe the impact that faster international transport links has on communicable disease.

  • The ripple of disease in a previously faraway land is now just a few hours on a long-haul flight, costing just a few hundred pounds.
  • An infection that starts in the Far East today is just hours from London, New York and Paris.

What is the World Health Organisation?

  • A specialised agency of the United Nations.
  • Its aim is the attainment by all peoples of the highest possible level of health.

Source PDF also misattributes “uphold international peace and security” to WHO in one bullet — that is the UN’s primary goal; WHO’s stated aim in the same slide is highest possible level of health.

Global health terminology

Recall the two classification systems used to group countries.

  • Human Development Index (HDI)
  • World Bank Classification

What is the Human Development Index (HDI)?

  • Approach used by the United Nations for quantifying development — a composite continuous value between 0.001 and 0.999.
  • Created to emphasise that people and their capabilities should be the ultimate criteria for assessing development, not economic growth alone.

Describe how HDI attributes a value to a country.

  • Healthy life: based around life expectancy
  • Knowledge: based on education and literacy
  • Standard of living: based on income adjusted for local circumstances (purchasing power parity, PPP)

What is the World Bank?

  • Set up in 1944; global partnership of 5 institutions with 189 member countries.
  • Core values: reduce poverty and promote sustainable development through financing and policy advice.

Describe how countries are classified using the World Bank Classification.

Based on Gross National Income (GNI) — arguably an objective measure of wealth.

Describe the classification levels within the World Bank Classification.

  • Low-income: GNI per capita of $1,135 or less
  • Lower middle-income: GNI per capita between $1,136 and $4,465
  • Upper-middle income: GNI per capita between $4,466 and $13,845
  • High-income: GNI per capita of $13,846 or more

What is the Global South?

  • Commonly used to denote countries where income tends to be low and economies are recently industrialising.
  • Most are around the tropics rather than in the southern hemisphere and have a colonial history. Australia and New Zealand are not considered part of the Global South.
  • Many are in Africa, Asia, and Latin America.

What is the Third World?

  • Cold War era: countries aligned with NATO = first world; aligned with USSR = second world.
  • Originally intended to denote non-aligned national state actors.

What are Less Economically Developed Countries (LEDCs)?

  • Vaguely used to categorise countries on the basis of GNI.
  • Term has now been removed from routine use by the World Bank.

Describe how terminology is changing in global health.

  • Discourse moving away from terms like third world and LEDC.
  • Commonly used: LMIC — low- and middle-income countries (includes low, lower-middle and upper-middle income per World Bank).

Global actors

What are Global Health systems?

  • Often described as a “constellation of actors” consisting of individuals and/or organisations whose primary purpose is to promote, restore and maintain health.
  • Institutional arrangements and relationships between actors are constantly changing.

Describe how global health actors are changing.

  • Traditional actors once thought to be WHO and national ministries of health.
  • Since the early 90s: influx of NGOs, Civil Society, private companies and philanthropic foundations challenging traditional actors.

SDGs and WASH

What are the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)?

A package of goals launched in 2015 by the United Nations following three years of consultation with national governments and their peoples — idea is to transform human progress at a global level.

How many SDGs are there and when were they designed to be achieved?

  • 17 goals total
  • Designed to be achieved within 15 years: 2015 to 2030

Describe the difference between SDGs and MDGs.

  • MDGs focused on LMIC contexts; SDGs are universal — as relevant for high-income countries as everywhere else.
  • Recognise interdependent environmental, economic and social challenges.
  • Message of leaving no one behind; tackling inequality of all types at their centre.

What is WASH?

  • WASH (Water, Sanitation and Hygiene) = SDG 6: ensuring availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all.
  • Collective term used due to interdependent nature of the three topics.

Explain the components of WASH.

  • Water: safe water for drinking, washing and domestic activities
  • Sanitation: safe removal of waste (toilets and waste disposal)
  • Hygiene: health promotion activities that encourage protective healthy behaviour practices

What proportion of the world’s population lacks access to safe drinking water and sanitation?

  • ~2.1 billion people (~30%) lack access to safely managed water
  • ~4.5 billion people (~60%) lack access to safely managed sanitation services
  • Unsafe hygiene practices also widespread with significant impact on morbidity and mortality

Recall three WASH-related diseases.

  • Diarrhoea
  • Malaria
  • Schistosomiasis (parasitic worms)
  • Trachoma (bacterial eye infection)
  • Intestinal helminths
  • Japanese encephalitis (viral, mosquito-borne)
  • Hepatitis A (liver infection spread in faeces of infected person)

Describe how improved WASH can improve diarrhoea outcomes.

  • Improved water supply reduces diarrhoea morbidity by 6–25%
  • Improved sanitation reduces diarrhoea morbidity by 32%
  • Hygiene interventions (e.g. hand washing education) can reduce diarrhoeal cases by up to 45%

Is safe drinking water a human right?

Yes. The United Nations declared safe drinking water and sanitation a human right in 2010.

What is safe drinking water?

Water that does not represent any significant threat to health over a lifetime of consumption.

Describe key considerations to ensure access to safe water and sanitation as a human right.

  • 50–100 litres of water per person per day needed for most basic needs
  • Water source within 1,000 metres from home
  • Water cost should not exceed 3% of household income
  • Collection time should not exceed 30 minutes

Describe the five main types of water-related infectious diseases.

  • Water-borne
  • Water-washed
  • Water-based
  • Water-related
  • Diseases caused by poor sanitation

What is SDG3?

“Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages”.

Describe the relationship between a country’s health outcomes and wealth and development.

  • Health = Wealth: as wealth/development increases → positive impacts on population health; as population health improves → positive impacts on wealth (GNI) and development.
  • NOTE: Wealth of a country may not reflect equitable spread between individuals; how health systems structure equity of access and cost has large impacts on individual health.

Planetary health

What is meant by planetary health?

A field that explores the interconnectedness of human health and the health of the planet’s natural systems.

What is the name given to the time period we are living in and what does it mean?

  • The Anthropocene
  • Human activity has started to have a significant impact on Earth’s ecosystems and climate

What is the ecological paradox?

Human development has progressed with positive improvements in human health, but planetary health itself is declining.

Describe the numerous impacts of climate change on health.

  • Extreme weather: flooding, fire etc. → death/injury; disrupts power and water
  • Water scarcity: rising temperatures and drought reduce fresh water
  • Food scarcity: heat, failing rains, loss of pollinators, soil degradation → crop failure, malnutrition
  • Migration and conflict: water/food insecurity and extreme weather → conflict and mass displacement
  • Heat- and pollution-related mortality: elderly and young particularly vulnerable; pollution worsened by rising temperatures → CVD and respiratory disease
  • Vector- and water-borne disease: geographic spread of mosquitoes/ticks; warmer waters increase cholera; drought/flooding contaminate water

What is the Wedding Cake Model for SDGs?

  • Highlights how planetary health is fundamental and the basis to ensuring health and well-being.
  • Shows a healthy biosphere as the foundation upon which we can build.
  • Describes how SDGs can be used to attain a healthy biosphere as well as economy and society.

Describe how climate change highlights health inequalities.

  • Burden of health effects not borne equally — those who contribute least to climate change will likely feel its worst effects.
  • Existing inequalities leave people more vulnerable, less able to recover, then worse off and more vulnerable — a vicious cycle.

What is famine?

Declared when malnutrition is widespread and people have started dying of starvation through lack of access to sufficient, nutritious food.

What is the biggest cause of famine?

  • Impact of “climate shocks” — unpredictable weather events exacerbated by climate change.
  • Other important, often inter-linking factors: economic instability and conflict.

What is an asylum seeker?

A person who has left their home country as a political refugee and is seeking asylum in another.

What is a refugee?

Someone forced to leave their country due to a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion.

What is an internally displaced person?

Source PDF question present with no expanded answer.

Standard definition: person forced to flee home but who remains within their country’s borders (unlike a refugee).

What is One Health?

  • An integrated, unifying approach to balance and optimise the health of people, animals and the environment.
  • Particularly important to prevent, predict, detect and respond to global health threats.
  • NOTE: Requires a multi-disciplinary approach with intersections between medicine, veterinary medicine and environmental sectors.
