An introduction to descriptive epidemiology

What is observational research?

Observational research involves observing and recording behaviours or phenomena in their natural setting without any manipulation or intervention by the researcher, aiming to describe, explore, or understand a specific phenomenon.

What is interventional research?

Interventional research (experimental research / clinical trials) involves researchers actively manipulating or assigning interventions (like treatments or procedures) to participants to determine their effects on health outcomes.

What is descriptive epidemiology?

  • Used to describe a problem often at an aggregated level.
  • Can be used to inform later analytic research and “often forms the first steps of our investigatory process as disease detectives”.

What is analytic epidemiology?

Used to deploy and test hypotheses, often at a person-level through which association can be measured and causation inferred.

Describe the difference between descriptive and analytic epidemiology.

Descriptive epidemiology is often used more in basic public health practice.

What is a statistic?

A statistic is a fixed value, derived from a sample, that estimates the value in the population.

What is a parameter?

  • A parameter is a fixed, often unknown value, which describes an entire population.
  • NOTE: A parameter is often unknown because it cannot be truly known unless you survey the entire population, which is often unfeasible.

Describe the difference between a statistic and a parameter.

Statistics are estimates drawn from samples that seek to estimate a population parameter.

What are point estimates?

  • A point estimate is a single value used to estimate an unknown population parameter.
  • It is a statistic, and it doesn’t show uncertainty — which is why it is often paired with confidence intervals.

What is meant by a 95% confidence interval?

  • A confidence interval is a range of values between which you are sure that your statistic/parameter in question lies.
  • A 95% confidence interval means you are 95% sure that your statistic lies between the upper and lower ends of the confidence interval.

What is a case report?

  • A case report is a detailed description of a single patient’s medical case.
  • In the modern day, it is often used for new/unknown diseases or unusual presentations of a known disease.

What is a case series?

A case series is a compilation of case reports.

What is a cross-sectional study?

  • Typically describes the prevalence of a condition across a population at a single point in time.
  • Useful where you may not have the time or money to find something out in detail.
  • A survey is an example of a cross-sectional study.

Describe the limitations of cross-sectional studies.

  • Limited because they are a single snapshot at a moment in time that often lacks context.
  • Techniques such as benchmarking (comparing to other organisations or national averages) can help compare out-of-context statistics and gain an idea of significance.

What is temporality in epidemiology?

  • Temporality is the requirement that an exposure (or cause) must precede an outcome (or effect) in time to establish a causal relationship.
  • Cross-sectional studies make it difficult to establish temporality because they are a single snapshot — we cannot observe the relationship between exposures and outcomes over time.

What is a longitudinal study?

  • Descriptive longitudinal studies describe the prevalence or incidence of an exposure or outcome over time.
  • For example, two identical cross-sectional studies taken over time can form a longitudinal study.
  • NOTE: The term longitudinal is often loosely applied to any study — descriptive or analytic — that involves measurement at more than one time-point.

What is an ecological study?

  • Ecological studies compare groups (rather than individuals).
  • They can be cross-sectional or longitudinal, and descriptive or analytic.

Ecological studies and categorising designs

When are ecological studies useful?

When we cannot collect data for all the individuals within a population.

What is ecological fallacy / aggregation bias?

The assumption that associations between groups hold for individuals.

Example: ecological fallacy / aggregation bias.

  • Increased chocolate consumption is associated with an increased number of Nobel Laureates — but association ≠ causation.
  • We cannot assume that increased chocolate consumption increases one’s chances of winning a Nobel Laureate, because we have not discovered the variations or context behind the individuals who won.
  • Making assumptions without accounting for these individuals would be ecological fallacy / aggregation bias.

Why use an ecological study if you will still have to conduct research on an individual level?

  • Ecological studies typically use secondary data sources that are already available.
  • They are relatively cheap and quick to complete.

Aggregated vs person-level data.

  • Aggregated data: for example, 5% of the population died.
  • Person-level data: for example, participants 1, 7 and 15 died.

What is primary data?

Primary data are those that are collected by the researcher first-hand.

Describe the advantages of primary data.

Primary data are fantastic because they will have been collected for a pre-specified purpose: to test the hypotheses or answer the research question(s) set by the researcher.

Describe the disadvantages of primary data.

  • Comes at significant cost — both financial and in terms of time.
  • Collecting (and cleaning) data is hugely time-consuming and often takes a very large proportion of a research project’s duration and budget.

What is secondary data?

Secondary data is the term applied to data that have been collected for another purpose — and then potentially ‘recycled’ for a different purpose.

Describe the advantages of secondary data.

Secondary data analyses are usually faster and cheaper to undertake.

Describe the disadvantages of secondary data.

  • May have to make a series of assumptions because the data analysed weren’t intended for the new purpose.
  • This may introduce critical limitations on how findings are interpreted.

Describe what is meant by routinely and non-routinely collected data.

  • Routinely collected data — e.g. census undertaken every ten years.
  • Non-routinely collected data — surveys and other bespoke datasets.

“Non-routinely collected data are the corollary to primary data” — what does this mean?

  • They are the opposite counterpart / alternative to primary data — like a mirror concept.
  • Primary data = collected deliberately and systematically for a research question.
  • Non-routinely collected data = opportunistic or ad-hoc data from other sources not originally meant for your study.

What is data linkage?

  • Joining two or more datasets together and, in doing so, finding out more than was possible by analysis of either original dataset alone.
  • With modern computing power and statistical packages, data linkage is now possible at a scale and speed never before possible.
  • NOTE: There are lawful and technical challenges that prevent data linkage from being implemented on a national scale in the UK — but it can help discover a lot of trends.

Cross-sectional studies revisited

What is a cross-sectional study? (analytic framing)

  • Describes the prevalence of a condition across a population at a single point in time (a snapshot).
  • Described as a snapshot because you observe the exposure and the outcome at the same moment in time.
  • Each individual is only assessed once and there is no follow-up.
  • Useful where you may not have the time or money to find something out in detail.
  • A survey is an example.
