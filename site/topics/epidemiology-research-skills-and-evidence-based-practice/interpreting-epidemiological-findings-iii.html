Confounding in epidemiological studies

What is confounding?

  • The effect of an extraneous variable that wholly or partially accounts for the apparent effect of the study exposure, or that masks an underlying true association.
  • Confounding leads to biased estimates and produces misleading results.

Example: confounding (birth order, maternal age, Down’s syndrome).

  • Graph 1: association between higher birth order and babies with Down’s syndrome (affected babies per 1,000 live births rises with birth order).
  • There appears to be higher risk at higher birth order — but is it birth order that increases this risk?
  • Graph 2: association between maternal age and Down’s syndrome (risk rises sharply with older maternal age, especially after 35).
  • Key takeaway: maternal age is related to birth order — maternal age confounds the birth-order association.
  • Association ≠ Causation.

Recall the four ways to identify confounding.

  1. Knowledge of the subject matter
  2. The three conditions for confounding: (1) associated with the exposure in the source population; (2) associated with the outcome in the absence of the exposure; (3) not a consequence of the exposure
  3. Stratification: stratify by variable of interest; compare stratum-specific estimates with the pooled estimate — a considerable difference indicates confounding
  4. Compare crude and adjusted estimate: if adjusted odds ratio differs from the crude odds ratio by 15% or more this may indicate confounding (adjusted estimate via techniques such as stratification). Not the optimal method of identification.

NOTE: You only need one of these methods to identify confounding.

Effect modification

What is effect modification?

Sometimes, after controlling for confounding and bias, there is a third variable which can impact on the association between exposure and outcome. This is called effect modification.

When does effect modification exist?

  • When the strength of the association varies over different levels of a third variable.
  • NOTE: Effect modification is a naturally occurring phenomenon and therefore should not be controlled for — stratified analysis should be undertaken. Reporting of results should account for it (e.g. results presented stratified by sex).

Describe the tests that can be used to identify whether the variable behind effect modification is actually significant.

  • Breslow–Day test
  • Q test
  • Interaction terms in regression models
  • Data is stratified by variable of interest; compare stratified specific estimates with the pooled estimate — a considerable difference indicates significance.

What is a synergistic effect modifier?

A synergistic effect modifier will potentiate the effects of the exposure.

What is an antagonistic effect modifier?

An antagonistic effect modifier will diminish the effects of the exposure.

Describe the difference between effect modification and confounding.

  • Confounding: a third variable distorts the true association between exposure and outcome — creates a false or misleading association; you want to control for it.
  • Effect modification: the effect of the exposure on the outcome actually changes across levels of a third variable — it’s real, not a distortion; you don’t control for it — you report it.

Interpreting epidemiological findings

What is the crude model?

The crude model is the univariate analysis of exposure vs outcome.
