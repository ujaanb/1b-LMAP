Prospective cohort studies and relative risk

Describe a major difference between descriptive and analytic studies.

  • Descriptive studies generally deal with groups.
  • Analytic studies generally deal with individuals.

What is a cohort study?

  • Typically involves a group of people without disease who are observed over a period of time to see what happens to them.
  • Also known as a longitudinal study.

What is the defining characteristic of a cohort study?

You track people forward in time from exposure to outcome.

Describe things that need to be considered when selecting a target population for cohort studies.

  • If studying a chronic disease associated with ageing, you may want to restrict your target population.
  • The exposure of interest may be rare, so you may need to target a specific population.
  • Should initially attempt to identify as many subjects as possible without invoking any restrictions.

Describe how exposures can be assessed in cohort studies.

  • Self-report
  • Taking physical measurements
  • Using existing records

Describe how outcomes can be assessed in cohort studies.

  • Routine surveillance
  • Death certificates
  • Medical records
  • Directly from the participant
  • NOTE: The method used to ascertain the outcome must be identical for both the exposed and the unexposed groups.

What is relative risk?

Relative risk quantifies how much more or less likely exposed individuals are to experience the outcome compared to unexposed individuals.

Relative risk = (Incidence in the exposed) / (Incidence in the unexposed)

What is a historical cohort study?

  • A group of individuals form the cohort, with a distribution of exposures and outcomes which are measured contemporaneously or extracted from health records.
  • You have the exposure and the outcome, but you are trying to find a link between the two.
  • Typically lower quality than prospective cohort studies because there is a greater risk of both selection and information biases.

Describe how we decide whether to include patients that are lost to follow-up.

There is no single right answer, but two guiding principles:

  1. Be transparent: participants lost to follow-up must be reported as such. Loss is almost unavoidable, but where large proportions are lost, consider study quality and whether the loss represents potential bias.
  2. Be conservative: if there’s potential for the loss to bias away from the null hypothesis, reporting the output statistic which biases towards the null is generally more sensible.

Questions to ask: is it reasonable to group those lost to follow-up with those we know didn’t experience the outcome? Should those lost be dropped from the denominator entirely?

Randomised controlled trials

What are randomised controlled trials?

  • Experimental studies which compare and assess the effectiveness of two or more treatments to see if one treatment is better than another.
  • Before a prescribed treatment can be used, it should always be rigorously tested using clinical trials.
  • Treatments being tested could be compared with no treatment, ideally using a placebo as the control.

Describe a limitation of randomised controlled trials.

  • While RCTs are the gold standard for evaluating the impact of an intervention on an outcome, randomisation alone does not wholly protect against bias.
  • REMEMBER: You must account for missing trial participants and include all participants in your analysis and in their original groups, regardless of whether or not they followed that allocation intervention.

What is “intention to treat” analysis compared to “per protocol” analysis?

  • Intention to treat is more tolerant of anomalies than per protocol analysis — used to mirror actual practice and see how clinically effective drugs are, including those who may not take the course correctly. It acknowledges that the intention to treat may not have happened for one reason or another.
  • Per protocol typically contains “cleaner” data as it removes anomalies and focuses on those who followed instructions / kept to protocol.

What is meant by random allocation in randomised controlled trials?

  • Random allocation of individuals to receive one of two or more interventions.
  • All participants have an equal chance of assignment to each available intervention.
  • Allocation is not determined by the investigator, clinicians or participants, and the allocation is not predictable.

Describe the two steps involved in random allocation.

  1. Generation of allocation sequence
  2. Implementation of the allocation (requires allocation concealment)

Describe some methods used to generate allocation sequences.

  • Simple randomisation
  • Using a random numbers table or computer software that generates the random sequence (as long as the number of participants is large enough)

What is simple randomisation?

Participants are assigned to different treatment groups randomly, ensuring each participant has an equal chance of being assigned to any group — often using methods like flipping a coin or a random number generator.

What is allocation concealment?

Ensuring that the person enrolling participants into a study is unaware of the upcoming treatment assignment, preventing selection bias.

Why is allocation concealment important?

  • Knowledge of the next assignment could influence whether a patient is included or excluded based on perceived prognosis (selection bias).
  • Important for preventing selection bias in randomised clinical trials.

Describe the two methods of allocation concealment.

  • Sequentially numbered opaque envelopes
  • Central randomisation

Describe how sequentially numbered opaque envelopes work.

  • Envelopes concerning treatment allocation are opened by the recruiting clinician on participant enrolment (e.g. drug A or B).
  • Envelopes should be truly opaque, sequentially numbered (001, 002, 003…), and opened in the correct order.
  • Given to patients in sequentially numbered order so the clinician has no idea which patient will get which allocation.
  • Clinicians should not open envelopes in advance and should ensure the seal has not been broken.

What is central randomisation?

  • Investigator enrols a participant then telephones a central randomisation service which issues a treatment allocation.
  • Addresses the problem of see-through or tampered envelopes which can cause selection bias.

Challenges in experimental trials

What is blinding?

  • A procedure whereby one or more parties in a trial are kept unaware of which treatment arm participants have been assigned to.
  • Performed to avoid and prevent conscious or unconscious bias in the design and delivery of a clinical trial.

Recall four sources of bias in a trial.

  • Patient being treated
  • Clinical staff administering the treatment
  • Physician assessing (the effects of) the treatment
  • Team interpreting results

Recall two types of blinding.

  • Single blinding — usually only the participant
  • Double blinding

What is performance bias?

  • Bias introduced when participants or researchers know which intervention group the participant is in, and that knowledge influences the care provided or behaviour during the trial.
  • Systematic differences between groups in care provided, or in exposure to factors other than the exposure of interest.

What is detection bias?

  • Systematic differences between groups in how outcomes are assessed/determined.
  • Example: a doctor knows who took which drug, and so administers tests/exams for this group subconsciously.
  • It’s a problem when outcomes are subjective.

What is the power of a study?

Covered fully in lecture 1.6: the ability to detect an effect or association if one truly exists. Typically we use 80% or 90% power.
