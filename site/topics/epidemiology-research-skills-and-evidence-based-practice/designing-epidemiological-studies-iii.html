What is the power of a study?

  • The ability to detect an effect or an association if one truly exists.
  • Typically we use 80% or 90% power.

How does changing your power affect the sample size?

Increasing the power increases the sample size.

How does changing the size of the ‘effect of interest’ affect the sample size?

Increasing the size of the effect of interest decreases the sample size.

If 20% of your sample drops out over the study period (are lost to follow-up), how could you mitigate this risk?

Intention-to-treat analysis.

What are the trade-offs and how do you balance the risks arising of Type I and Type II error?

  • If false positives are more dangerous (e.g. approving a harmful drug), keep alpha low (e.g. 0.01). Decreasing alpha reduces the risk of a false positive, as a higher degree of certainty is required to reject the null.
  • If missing a real effect is more harmful (e.g. detecting early signs of a deadly disease), tolerate a higher alpha and focus on higher power. Increasing power reduces the risk of a false negative. If power is really high and still no association is found, it is likely there isn’t one.

What is alpha?

  • The study’s alpha is how much you want to rule out chance causing a positive finding: equivalent to specifying the p-value, and connected with one- and two-tailed testing.
  • Decreasing alpha from 0.05 to 0.01 will increase your sample size.
  • Alpha is the probability of a Type I error — finding a difference when a difference does not exist.
  • Most medical literature uses an alpha cut-off of 5% (0.05) — indicating a 5% chance that a significant difference is actually due to chance (false positive).
