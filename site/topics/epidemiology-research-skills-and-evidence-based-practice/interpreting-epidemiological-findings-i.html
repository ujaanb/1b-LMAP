1.7 Interpreting Epidemiological Findings I

Narrative vs systematic reviews, structured search, screening, grey literature, Cochrane, meta-analysis, forest plots, heterogeneity, and publication bias.

Sections

Systematic and non-systematic reviews

What is a literature review?

A literature review is a critical summary and evaluation of existing research on a specific topic.

Recall the two types of literature review.

  • Narrative review
  • Literature review (systematic / structured forms are contrasted in the lecture)

What is a narrative review?

  • Brings together the published literature into a single article, enabling the reader to rapidly understand the issues.
  • Sometimes referred to as a literature review, scoping review, or non-systematic review.
  • The published literature collated into a single article is chosen by the researcher.

Describe the strengths of narrative reviews.

  • Agile — normally easier and faster to write; may often be more up to date than the latest ‘Systematic Review’.
  • Particularly useful when looking at areas with limited research or higher levels of variation in research approaches.
  • Can be very useful where work from different disciplines is being brought together, with a less easily answerable research question.

Describe the limitations of narrative reviews.

  • Subject to potential bias as the author is free to select works that may support their opinion (although ethical authors and robust peer review should prevent overly-speculative or unbalanced reviews).
  • With no search being specified, it is possible that important evidence is omitted by chance (rather than intent).

What is a systematic review?

  • Sets out a highly structured approach to searching, sifting, including and summarising the literature — the researcher does not choose the literature that is published.
  • Often presented as the basis for meta-analysis, but also exists separately.
  • Sometimes described as one of the strongest forms of evidence.

Describe the strengths of systematic reviews.

  • Aims to collate all available evidence that relates to a highly focused research question.
  • Implements a highly specified protocol that enables reproducibility — usually involving a structured search.
  • Whether or not evidence is included is based on pre-specified criteria: inclusion criteria.

What is a structured search?

A method that uses specific keywords, operators and filters to look for literature, and enables transparency so future researchers can reproduce the approach.

What are indices?

  • Often refers to indexed databases — places where studies are catalogued and searchable (e.g. PubMed).
  • It is always made clear which search indices were used and the time period involved.

What is screening?

  • The process of reviewing titles, abstracts, and full texts of studies retrieved during your literature search to determine which studies to include and which to exclude.
  • Usually documented using a PRISMA flow diagram showing: number of records found; how many were excluded at each stage; final number of studies included.

Describe the limitations of systematic reviews.

  • Can take many months to design the search, review all sources (often thousands) and synthesise findings.
  • Only as good as the method (search system) employed — a less-than-comprehensive search may not return all evidence.
  • Only as good as the indices searched.
  • Only as good as the evidence that it incorporates.
  • Very quickly out of date — often exacerbated by the delay in publishing. TIP: look at the search date, not the publication date!

What is grey literature?

  • Research and information that’s not published in traditional academic journals — not controlled by commercial or scholarly publishers (e.g. government reports not found in indices like PubMed).
  • Highlights that not everything that is known is necessarily in the published literature.
  • Particularly a challenge in public health and global health, where many implementations and programmes might be reported with varying degrees of accuracy.

What is the Cochrane collaboration?

Seeks a world of improved health where decisions about health and health care are informed by high-quality, relevant and up-to-date synthesised research evidence.

Meta-analysis

What is meta-analysis?

A quantitative, formal, epidemiological study design used to systematically assess previous research studies to derive conclusions about that body of research.

What is a Forest plot?

  • The way in which we communicate the findings of a meta-analysis.
  • A graphical way to show the results of multiple studies on the same topic, and how they combine to form an overall effect.

How do you interpret the diamond on a forest plot?

If the diamond for the total line does not cross the no-effect line, the overall result is statistically significant.

Describe the condition that needs to be met for meta-analysis to be valid.

Meta-analysis requires that the pooled studies are sufficiently similar — or else your results are meaningless.

Describe the sources of heterogeneity in meta-analyses.

  • Clinical: patients, selection criteria
  • Methodological: study design, blinding, intervention approach
  • Statistical: reporting differences
  • NOTE: As a reader, understand the degree of heterogeneity and how authors communicate what that may mean for the findings.

What is meant by fixed and random effects?

  • Weighting of studies included in a meta-analysis is mostly proportional to study size; we can also build in assumptions that account for other differences and similarities:
  • Fixed effects
  • Random effects
  • Applying each has different impacts on weighting and hence findings.

What is publication bias?

  • Tendency for studies with positive or significant results to be more likely to be published than studies with negative, inconclusive, or null results.
  • A publication funnel plot can show the balance of evidence between studies assuming an overall effect size: more publications on one side of the line may suggest that some studies may not have been reported.

Describe the limitations of meta-analysis.

Can be limited by the quality of the studies included, the statistical methods employed, and is often only possible after multiple interventional or observational studies have been completed.
