Welcome to epidemiology and the burden of disease

What does it mean for a disease to be endemic?

  • Endemic refers to diseases that reside within a population.
  • A disease that is consistently present within a particular geographic area or population.

What does it mean for a disease to be epidemic?

  • Epidemic refers to diseases that befall a population.
  • A disease whose prevalence suddenly increases beyond what is normally expected in a particular area or group.

What is epidemiology?

The study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.

In other words: how often diseases occur in different groups of people and why.

Describe the importance of epidemiology.

  • It enables us to prevent disease by making the correct decisions.
  • If we know why a disease has a certain distribution in a population, we can potentially prevent it with appropriate interventions.

How many types of prevention are there?

Three.

Recall the three types of prevention.

  • Primary
  • Secondary
  • Tertiary

What is primary prevention?

  • Primary prevention is the prevention of disease through the control of exposure to risk factors.
  • Example: reducing salt intake to prevent risk of hypertension.

Describe in one sentence the aim of primary prevention.

Primary prevention is before the onset of disease.

What is secondary prevention?

  • Secondary prevention is the application of available measures to detect early departures from good health and to introduce appropriate treatment and interventions.
  • Example: controlling hypertension with antihypertensive drugs.
  • NOTE: Secondary prevention is all about detecting and treating early disease.

Describe in one sentence the aim of secondary prevention.

Secondary prevention slows progression.

Source PDF mislabels this prompt as “aim of primary prevention” in one place; answer content is for secondary prevention.

What is tertiary prevention?

  • Tertiary prevention is the application of measures to reduce or eliminate long-term impairments and disabilities caused by existing departures from good health, minimising suffering and promoting the patient’s adjustments to their condition.
  • Example: rehabilitation after stroke to restore pre-morbid functioning.

Describe in one sentence the aim of tertiary prevention.

Tertiary prevention enables return to functioning after insult.

Describe the six main principles of epidemiology.

  • Curiosity — that doesn’t sound right!
  • Problem solving — how could I explain that result?
  • Numerical ability — what do I need to measure to figure this out?
  • Critical thinking — what might I be getting wrong?
  • Communication — how do I explain this to people who aren’t epidemiologists?
  • Creativity — on the basis of what I think is going on, what do we do next?

Describe how we are guided in epidemiological investigations.

Guiding questions: What? Why? When? How? Where? Who?

These emphasise three principles that are very important in epidemiology:

  • Time
  • Person
  • Place

Exposures and outcomes

What is an exposure?

  • An exposure is essentially the variable that we are trying to associate with a change in health status.
  • Example: in a study of cancer outcomes, Drug X (the exposure) and mortality at five years (the outcome).

What is an outcome?

An outcome is the result or effect of a particular exposure, intervention, or event on health status.

What is a characteristic that we typically desire outcomes to have?

  • Outcomes should usually be continuous as well.
  • Example: you’d rather measure blood pressure values rather than hypertensive? (YES or NO).

Describe the general relationship between exposures and outcomes.

Usually, your exposure will be the independent variable and the outcome will be the dependent variable.

Epidemiologic transition and populations

What is the demographic transition model?

A model that describes how the birth and death rates of a population change over time as a country develops, transitioning from high birth and death rates to low birth and death rates.

What is the epidemiological transition model?

  • A progression of the demographic transition model that uses data such as birth rate, death rate and life expectancy to predict the challenges a population may face at a particular moment in time.
  • It highlights how society has seen a change in the primary causes of death from infectious diseases → chronic and degenerative diseases.

What is the first stage of the epidemiologic transition model?

Pestilence and famine.

Describe the characteristics of the first stage.

  • Rapid urbanisation
  • Constraints on food supply
  • High birth rate and high mortality
  • Life expectancy low at birth

Recall the UK period mapped to the first stage.

Pre-1800s.

What is life expectancy?

The average length of time one can expect to live based on the rest of the population.

What is the second stage?

Receding pandemics.

Recall the UK period mapped to the second stage.

1800s–1950s.

Describe the characteristics of the second stage.

  • Agricultural development improves nutrition
  • Life expectancy increases
  • Water, sanitation and hygiene
  • Vaccination emerges
  • High birth rate and reducing mortality

What is the third stage?

Degenerative and man-made diseases.

Recall the UK period mapped to the third stage.

1950s–2010s.

Describe the characteristics of the third stage.

  • Lifestyle factors and non-communicable diseases dominate (cancer and cardiovascular disease)
  • Environmental and global determinants drive obesity and other risk factors
  • Technology reduces need for physical labour
  • Addiction, violence and other issues emerge

What is the fourth stage?

Delayed degenerative diseases and emerging infections.

Describe the characteristics of the fourth stage.

  • Health technology defers morbidity, albeit at increasing financial cost
  • Emerging threat of zoonotic disease
  • Inequalities within and between countries come to the fore

Recall the UK period mapped to the fourth stage.

Post-2010s.

Epidemiology in the history of HIV

What was the role of epidemiology in the history of HIV?

  1. Describing a consistent constellation of signs and symptoms
  2. Identifying the causative pathogen
  3. Inferring the mechanism(s) of transmission
  4. Determining the risk factors associated with disease transmission and prognosis
  5. Informing prevention strategies
  6. Evaluating the efficacy and effectiveness of therapies (including drugs and other interventions)

What is a case series?

A document comprising multiple case reports drawn together.

Conclusion

Epidemiology is the language of population health and is central to applying the principles of evidence-based medicine. It gives you a language.
