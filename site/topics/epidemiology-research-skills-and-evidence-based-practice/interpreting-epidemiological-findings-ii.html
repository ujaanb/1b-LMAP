1.8 Interpreting Epidemiological Findings II

Association vs causation, Bradford-Hill criteria, correlation, internal/external validity, and selection bias.

Sections
  1. Association and causation
  2. Validity and bias

Association and causation

Describe the relationship between association and causation.

Association ≠ Causation.

What is the Bradford-Hill criteria?

  • Enable us to infer causation from both observational and interventional methods.
  • NOTE: Taken together, they will not yield a simple threshold or yes/no answer to causation, but are used carefully and with considerable difficulty to infer causation.

Describe the components of the Bradford-Hill criteria.

  • Strength
  • Consistency
  • Specificity
  • Temporality
  • Biological Gradient
  • Plausibility
  • Coherence
  • Experiment
  • Analogy

Describe the importance of strength.

  • A stronger association increases the confidence that an exposure causes an outcome.
  • Example: Hill highlighted the 200-fold increase in scrotal cancer among chimney sweeps attributed to tar and mineral oils.
  • The 9- or 10-fold increase in lung cancer risk with tobacco was suggestive of causality.

Describe the importance of consistency.

  • Repeatability of findings by time, person and place is suggestive of causation.
  • Consistent findings across settings tended to rule out errors or fallacies that might befall one or two studies.

Describe the importance of specificity.

  • Describes an association between specific causes and specific effects.
  • NOTE: Specificity can be informative when present, but its absence conveys very little.

Describe the importance of temporality.

It is insufficient for Exposure A and Outcome B to co-exist: A must precede B.

Describe the importance of biological gradient.

  • A dose-response effect (in the ‘right’ direction) is a compelling argument for causality.
  • Example: smoking — higher risk among smokers is bolstered by more cigarettes conferring greater risk.
  • NOTE: Quantification is often difficult. When present, suggestive; absence may be of no value.

Describe the importance of biological plausibility.

The relationship should be biologically plausible where the science is ‘understood’.

Describe the importance of coherence.

The association ought to be consistent with the existing theory and knowledge.

Describe the importance of experimentation.

Evidence from experimentation should be supportive of the proposed link.

Describe the importance of analogy.

  • Drawing upon analogous findings, we may make inference on the relationship.
  • Example: because Rubella causes deafness, eye and heart problems, it is reasonable to assume other viral illnesses in pregnancy may render similar pathology (Hill).
  • Example: because thalidomide caused fetal abnormalities (phocomelia), it is rational to suspect antenatal drugs may cause similar problems (Hill).

What is correlation?

A statistical term that's often incorrectly used as a synonym of association, but describes a linear relationship between two variables.

Validity and bias

What is internal validity?

  • The degree to which the design and conduct of a study allow you to draw accurate conclusions about a causal relationship between variables.
  • Are the study’s findings representative of the ‘truth’?

What is external validity?

  • How well the results of a study can be applied to or generalised beyond the specific study setting (to other people, places, times, or conditions).
  • Are the study’s findings generalisable to similar situations?
  • NOTE: External validity is sometimes referred to as generalisability.

What is bias?

  • Any trend in the collection, analysis, interpretation, publication or review of data that can lead to conclusions that are systematically different from the truth.
  • A systematic error in the design or conduct of a study can result in bias.
  • NOTE: Bias is always due to systematic error, not random error (which usually cancels itself out). Systematic errors → bias can affect the validity of a study.

Describe the three main categories of bias.

  • Selection bias
  • Information bias
  • Confounding

What is selection bias?

  • Occurs when the people or things included in a study or analysis are not representative of the larger population intended to be analysed.
  • Can occur when an individual’s chances of being included are related to BOTH the exposure and the outcome — leading to a biased interpretation of the relationship.
  • NOTE: Case-control studies are particularly susceptible to selection bias.
