Introduction to quantitative epidemiology

Components of qualitative epidemiology.

  • Explores underlying ideas and themes to inform research questions and possible future hypotheses.
  • Expresses its findings and outputs in words.
  • Relies on smaller numbers of participants, but goes into substantial detail.
  • Often used earlier in the research process — and gives you a place to start.

The burden of disease

What’s the third group of conditions (that’s not communicable or non-communicable disease)?

Injuries.

What is a Disability-adjusted life year (DALY)?

  • DALYs measure the overall burden of disease by calculating the number of years lost due to disability, ill-health, or early death.
  • One DALY represents the loss of one year of full health.

Describe why morbidity is equally as important as mortality in health.

  • Mortality and morbidity are very different things; as a medical profession we mustn’t be driven purely by what kills people. Quality of life matters.
  • That back and neck pain are significant causes of morbidity is an incredibly important insight.
  • The size of the burden of neck and back pain has contributed to factors leading to the opioid and pain-killer epidemic seen here and in the United States.

Distribution of disease between demographics.

  • Disease is distributed differently between genders and age groups.
  • We often focus on cancer, dementia and CVD among older people, and congenital disorders and childhood cancers among the very young — but most people are between these poles. Supporting positive behaviours in this group will pay dividends in later life.

Describe the importance of tackling mental health issues.

  • There is no health without mental health.
  • Self-harm, substance misuse, anxiety and depressive disorders are hugely important causes of both morbidity and mortality.
  • Mental health is not merely a nice-to-have, but is arguably far more important than more trendy medical innovations such as robotic surgery or targeted cancer therapies.

Measures of frequency: odds, prevalence and incidence

What are measures of frequency?

  • Statistical measures used to quantify the frequency of discrete variables occurring or not.
  • NOTE: Continuous variables often have summary measures such as mean, median, mode or range.

Recall the four main measures of frequency.

  • Odds
  • Prevalence
  • Cumulative incidence
  • Incidence rate

What are odds?

  • Odds is the ratio of the probability (P) of an event to its complement (1 − P).
  • Here the term ratio describes the number obtained by dividing one quantity by another.

Describe the formula for odds, using the possession of a disease as an example.

Odds = (Number of people with the disease) / (Number of people who don’t have the disease)

What is prevalence?

The proportion of individuals in a population who have the attribute at a specific timepoint.

Describe the formula for prevalence, using the possession of a disease as an example.

Prevalence = (Number of people with the disease) / (Total number of individuals in the population)

What is the significance of a 0% prevalence of a disease?

No one in the population has the disease.

What is the significance of a 100% prevalence of a disease?

Everyone in the population has the disease.

A limitation of prevalence

A limitation of prevalence is that it doesn’t take into account new cases of a disease.

What is external validity?

External validity is the degree to which the findings of a study can be applied to other situations, people, settings, and measures. It’s a judgment-based concept that’s not calculated as a statistic.

What is cumulative incidence?

The proportion of the population with a new event during a given time period.

i.e. incidence being number of new cases.

Describe the formula for cumulative incidence, using the possession of a disease as an example.

Cumulative incidence = (Number of new cases during the period of interest) / (Number of disease-free individuals at the start of this time period)

NOTE: The denominator for cumulative incidence normally only refers to the proportion of individuals that are susceptible to the disease.

What is the significance of a 0 (0%) cumulative incidence of a disease?

There were no new cases during the study period.

What is the significance of a 1 (100%) cumulative incidence of a disease?

All individuals developed the disease during the study period.

Recall two alternative terms for cumulative incidence.

  • Incidence proportion
  • Risk

Describe the conditions that must be met for cumulative incidence data to be valid.

  • There must be a follow-up period in the study
  • This follow-up period must be the same for all participants
  • No new participants being allowed to enter the group

Describe the issue that incidence rate addresses as a measure of frequency.

  • Sometimes subjects are lost to follow-up and participants enter or leave the study population at any time.
  • Incidence rate accounts for this whereas cumulative incidence does not, by using the concept of person-time.

What is person-time?

  • Person-time is a measure of the time that a participant spends in a study.
  • During their person-time, the individual could have developed an event that would have been counted as a case.
  • NOTE: Every individual contributes person-time from the moment they enter to the moment they are diagnosed with the disease of interest, die, or are lost to follow-up (or until the study ends).

Describe the formula for incidence rate, using the possession of a disease as an example.

Incidence rate = (Number of new cases during the follow-up period) / (Total person-time by disease-free individuals)

When to prefer prevalence vs incidence measures (notes from questions).

  • You can use prevalence especially for chronic disease (e.g. hypertension), because the disease will likely remain for an extended period — a snapshot at any given time shouldn’t be a problem.
  • With transient diseases such as influenza, avoid prevalence as individuals may have caught the disease and recovered before your snapshot.
  • Prefer incidence rate over cumulative incidence for transient diseases such as influenza, as person-time is lost the moment they get the disease.

Adjusting for differences: standardisation

What is standardisation?

  • A statistical approach that allows us to draw comparisons.
  • A method used to make fair comparisons between two or more populations that may differ in important ways (e.g. age, sex, or other demographic factors).

Recall the two types of standardisation.

  • Direct standardisation
  • Indirect standardisation

What is meant by crude death rate?

The crude death rate measures the number of deaths in a particular population, scaled to the size of that population, per unit of time, usually expressed as deaths per 1,000 people.

What is direct standardisation?

  • Direct standardisation by age allows us to compare populations with different age demographics statistically and make reasoned analysis.
  • You use the same age-specific rates observed in your study, but multiply by a standard population structure (distribution) to scale it up accurately and adjust for differences (e.g. number of people in each age group).
  • Commonly used to compare two sets of data by standardising them against the same standard dataset.
  • It answers: “What would the overall rate be if both populations had the same age distribution?”

What is indirect standardisation?

  • A statistical method that compares the observed number of events in a population to the expected number of events based on a standard population.
  • A standardised mortality ratio (SMR) is calculated to compare death rates between populations: Observed / Expected deaths.
  • Useful when we only have high-level data about outcomes, or when age-specific rates are unreliable (small or unstable). Often the first step on a journey of enquiry.
  • You use the same population structure seen in your study, but multiply by standard age-specific rates to calculate an expected death value, then compare to observed deaths.

Describe the difference between direct and indirect standardisation.

  • Direct: study’s age-specific rates × standard population structure.
  • Indirect: study’s population structure × standard age-specific rates → expected deaths; compare with observed (SMR).

Worked example: indirect standardisation (Town A vs national).

Question: is mortality in Town A higher or lower than expected vs the national population?

Town A population: 0–14: 1,000; 15–64: 2,000; 65+: 500. Observed deaths = 15.

National death rates per 1,000: 0–14: 0.5; 15–64: 2.0; 65+: 10.0.

Expected deaths: 0–14 → 0.5; 15–64 → 4.0; 65+ → 5.0; total expected = 9.5.

SMR = Observed / Expected = 15 / 9.5 = 1.58
  • SMR = 1.58 → Town A had 58% more deaths than expected after adjusting for age.
  • Mortality is higher in Town A than expected if it followed national trends.
  • Recap: Town A’s age-specific death rates were not used; Town A’s age structure + total observed deaths + national age-specific rates were used.
