Life-course approach to child health

What is a life course approach to health?

Considers all physical, mental and wider determining factors of health and their contribution to health and wellbeing throughout an individual’s life, not at just one point in time — from pre-conception through early years, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, later life and end of life.

Describe the advantage of looking at health from the life course perspective.

Rather than a ‘disease-focused’ approach, we approach health more holistically and can take a more proactive role in promoting and restoring health and wellbeing at different stages — and in turn prevent ill health in the future.

Describe the importance of early years in a child’s development.

  • Foundations for virtually every aspect of human development — physical, intellectual and emotional — are laid in early childhood.
  • What happens during these early years, starting in the womb, has a lifelong effect on many aspects of health and well-being (obesity, heart disease, mental health, educational achievement, economic status).
  • Later interventions, although important, are considerably less effective when good foundations are lacking.

What is school readiness?

A child’s overall preparedness for school, encompassing physical, social, emotional and cognitive development. It is a marker linked to educational attainment, life chances and even involvement in crime.

Recall the prevalence of childhood obesity.

1 in 5 children are entering reception as overweight or obese.

Describe the clinical consequences of childhood obesity.

  • Overweight infants are five times more likely to be obese in later childhood, adolescence and adulthood.
  • Lifestyle-associated conditions such as Type II diabetes, fatty liver disease and arteriosclerosis are increasingly observed in children.

Describe the impact of physical activity on children.

Children develop physical literacy — the motivation, confidence, physical competence, knowledge and understanding to value physical activity throughout their life course.

Describe how important it is to cultivate healthy physical activity habits when young.

It is far easier to establish positive, healthy behaviour early in life than to change behaviour later when habit and inertia become more powerful.

Dental caries (Introducing Archie)

What are dental caries?

  • Occur when oral bacteria metabolise sugar and produce acid that demineralises enamel and dentin from the hard coating of the tooth.
  • Severe caries can cause pain and infection and can even lead to sepsis and death.

Describe the health burden of dental caries.

  • A major public health problem and the most common non-communicable disease globally.
  • Children and adolescents are most at risk; in 2022, just under a quarter of five-year-olds experienced dental caries in England.

Describe the factor that increases one’s likelihood of dental decay.

  • Children from the most deprived areas are more than twice as likely to experience tooth decay — social deprivation is associated with excessive sugar intake.
  • With increasing difficulty accessing NHS dentistry, these inequalities are likely to worsen.

Describe a condition commonly linked to dental caries.

Dental caries and obesity often occur together, further disproportionally affecting disadvantaged populations.

Describe the management of tooth decay in childhood.

Management may need to involve tooth extraction — extremely expensive, time consuming and widely unavailable in low-income countries.

Describe how tooth decay can be prevented in childhood.

  • Sugar reduction in the diet and maintaining good oral hygiene.
  • Reducing free sugar intake to less than 10% of energy intake can minimise risk throughout the life course.
  • Population-wide approaches: water fluoridation, fluoride varnish programmes and sugar-reduction programmes.

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs)

What is an Adverse Childhood Experience (ACE)?

Stressful events that occur in childhood.

Describe three examples of ACEs.

  • Experiencing abuse: physical, sexual, psychological or neglect
  • Witnessing domestic abuse
  • Having a close family member/caregiver who misused drugs or alcohol
  • Having a close family member/caregiver with mental health problems
  • Having a close family member/caregiver who served time in prison
  • Experiencing parental separation or divorce on account of relationship breakdown

Describe the best way to reduce the impact of ACEs.

Early intervention is preferable.

Describe the three principles of preventing the long-term effects of ACEs.

  • Reducing stress
  • Building responsive relationships
  • Strengthening life skills

Examples: strategies to prevent and mitigate ACE impacts.

  • Universal and selective services — home visits, parenting/family programmes (e.g. Family Nurse Partnership)
  • School-based social and emotional learning / PSHE to develop problem-solving, coping skills and resilience
  • Early identification of adversity and timely support for children with multiple adversities
  • Early cost-effective interventions and trauma-informed approaches (e.g. Trauma Recovery Model)

A whole-family approach

Describe three principles of lifestyle consultations with children and their families.

  • Be opportunistic (e.g. incorporate nutritional advice in other consultations)
  • Be sensitive
  • Non-judgmental approach
  • Strengths-based approach
  • Focus on barriers of particular importance to the child
  • Relate to other children
  • Engage parents and families
  • Specific, short-term goals
  • Part of daily routine

Should you recommend physical activity for children with asthma, diabetes, epilepsy or cancer?

  • Doctors typically shy away, when in fact there are a multitude of benefits for long-term conditions in children and physical activity can actually be safe and fun.
  • “Moving Medicine” online consultation guides can help tailor consultations.

Describe the role that schools play in promoting healthy behaviours.

  • Ranges from complying with legislation (e.g. School Food Regulations 2014) to proactively introducing initiatives like the Daily Mile.
  • The Daily Mile: primary schools/nurseries take children outside running for 15 minutes every day.

Describe a characteristic of recommendations that can make participation more likely.

Activities that fulfil the LEAP criteria (Local, Enjoyable and Practical) make participation more likely.

What is the National Child Measurement Programme (NCMP)?

Measures height and weight of children in reception (ages 4–5) and year six (ages 10–11). Assesses changes and trends over time in the population.

Describe how BMI results are interpreted in children.

  • BMI = weight (kg) / height² (m²).
  • Unlike adults, the BMI value is compared to reference measurements that take into account age and sex of the child.
  • Based on the BMI centile, the child is considered underweight, healthy weight, overweight, obese, or a subcategory (e.g. severely obese).

Child health & nutrition / microbiome

Food groups and relative prices.

Macronutrient-dense and micronutrient-poor foods are much cheaper per 1000 calories.

What is considered hidden hunger?

Chronically low intake or absorption of nutrients or minerals to sustain good health and development.

What is the microbiome and where is it mostly found?

  • Trillions of micro-organisms including bacteria, viruses, fungi and protozoa that exist both in and on our bodies — vital for maintaining health; usually symbiotic with human physiology and the environment.
  • Most of the microbiome is in the GI system. The large intestine constitutes over 70%; Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are the two main phyla (carbohydrate break-down).

What factors in a child’s very early life will affect their microbiome?

  • Mother’s microbiome during pregnancy — in utero exposure to microbial DNA and maternal microbes
  • Mode of delivery — vaginal: colonised by maternal vaginal organisms; caesarean: mostly maternal skin flora, less diverse/fewer gut bacteria early on
  • Breast milk vs formula feeding — significant gut microbiome differences; exposure to flora in nipple, ducts and areola

Describe the role of the gut microbiome in vitamin production.

Role in Vitamin K synthesis (clotting cascade) and synthesis of several components of vitamin B.

Describe the role of the gut microbiome in the immune response.

  • GI mucosal immune system must tolerate beneficial commensals while preventing pathogen overgrowth.
  • Pathogens: dendritic cells detect and initiate pro-inflammatory response (cytokines, T-cell activation).
  • Poor diet/unhealthy microbiome can lead to chronic low-level inflammatory state associated with chronic disease.
  • “Leaky gut” increases permeability — LPS crosses into bloodstream causing further inflammation.
  • Fermentation (esp. Firmicutes) releases SCFAs — energy source, lower colon pH; butyrate vital for immune homeostasis.

Describe the role of the gut microbiome in the nervous system.

  • Brain–gut axis: bi-directional relationship via enteric nervous system, immune system and vagus nerve.
  • Microbiome helps produce chemical neurotransmitters; pathways involved in inflammatory responses and gut motility.
  • Metabolises tryptophan, involved in serotonin (5-HT) production.

Physical activity and sleep

What is physical literacy and how can it be developed?

  • Motivation, confidence, physical competence, knowledge and understanding to value physical activity throughout the life course.
  • Developed when children engage in inclusive, positive, meaningful physical activity experiences that place equal value on physical and psychological benefits to health and wellbeing.

National sleep recommendations.

Not everyone needs the same amount of sleep — driven by genetics and dependent on life stage.

Describe the mechanism behind some people needing more sleep than others.

  • ~1% are naturally short sleepers and function well on as little as four hours.
  • Suggested genetic mutations affect orexin (hypocretin) production; increased orexin leads to increased wakefulness.

What is an ultradian rhythm?

Biological cycles that repeat within a 24-hour period. At birth, babies have ultradian rhythms.

Describe when the circadian system develops in infants and how.

  • Does not develop until about two months of age.
  • Suggested that breastfeeding may facilitate development as melatonin is passed on in mother’s milk.

What is the recommended upper limit for caffeine?

~400 mg per day.

Describe the effect of caffeine consumption too close to bedtime.

The half-life of caffeine is around five hours.
