Clinical approaches to nutrition in the older person

Describe how food intake changes as we age and why.

  • Naturally declines due to physiological changes including fall in basal metabolic rate and loss of lean body mass.
  • Reduced sensation of taste and smell makes food less appealing.

What is malnutrition and what are the two types?

  • Occurs when someone's diet doesn't contain the correct amounts of nutrients — NOT a normal part of ageing.
  • Can be undernutrition or overnutrition → obesity.

Describe physical changes that put older people at risk of malnutrition.

  • Poor dentition
  • Swallowing difficulty
  • Arthritis and declining co-ordination
  • Deteriorating eyesight
  • These make the practical aspect of eating difficult

Describe psychological changes that put older people at risk of malnutrition.

Low mood and cognitive decline can make it difficult to plan, buy and prepare meals.

Describe medical conditions that put older people at risk of malnutrition.

  • Cancers
  • Heart failure
  • CKD
  • Hypothyroidism
  • Coeliac disease
  • IBD/malabsorption
  • NOTE: Medications can also affect appetite (e.g. diuretics, anti-hypertensives)

Describe how older people are at higher risk of overnutrition.

Physical or cognitive problems can lead to reduced activity and, in turn, weight gain.

Describe how older people are at higher risk of malnutrition in institutionalised settings.

  • Many live in nursing homes dependent on staff for feeding.
  • If not done well, risk of malnutrition — most likely undernutrition.

Describe five effects of malnutrition.

  • Impaired immune function
  • Poor wound healing
  • Osteoporosis
  • Cognitive impairment
  • Mood disturbance
  • Joint and muscle pain

Describe effects of specific nutrient deficiencies.

  • Lack of calcium and vitamin D → osteomalacia
  • Lack of calcium and vitamin C → poor wound healing
  • Lack of calcium and vitamin B12 and folate → anaemia

Describe how skeletal muscle mass is affected in older people.

Sarcopenia — a component of frailty syndrome that increases morbidity and mortality.

Describe components of history in clinical assessment of malnutrition.

  • Changes to weight & BMI
  • Additional symptoms of malnutrition
  • Social history — living situation, care needs, functional status
  • Mental health
  • Drug history
  • PMH
  • Food and fluid diary — with family/carers if patient consents

Describe examination and investigation.

  • Exam: weight, BMI, dentition, other relevant areas
  • Investigations: consider blood tests; others guided by history and exam

What is the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST)?

Tool used to gain an objective indication of the risk of malnutrition.

Common sleep disorders

What is Obstructive Sleep Apnoea (OSA)?

A sleep-related breathing disorder resulting from a partial or fully blocked airway.

Describe what happens in OSA.

  • When airway blocked during sleep, brain starved of oxygen.
  • Creates a mini arousal to reinvigorate airway musculature → sharp intake of breath.

Describe the clinical presentation of OSA.

Often characterised by loud snoring, followed by a pause in breathing, then a large gasp of air.

What is Excessive Daytime Sleepiness?

  • Increased pressure to fall asleep during normal waking hours — clinical symptom of OSA.
  • Distinct from somnolence (strong desire to sleep, pathological or not) and from fatigue (physical/mental exhaustion not facilitating/alleviated by sleep).

Describe the consequences of OSA.

  • Increased risk of CVD, stroke, Type 2 diabetes independent of weight and age
  • More severe depression
  • Pauses lead to hypercapnia and cellular death

Describe how OSA is diagnosed or screened for.

  • Polysomnography is gold standard but expensive and resource intensive.
  • STOP-Bang questionnaire provides a useful quick tool for GPs to begin differential diagnosis.

Describe the causes of snoring and OSA.

  • Large tonsils
  • Jaw too far back — resolved by mandibular advancement
  • Excess weight — adipose at base of tongue/pharynx → airway collapse when muscles relaxed
  • Sleeping on back is a risk factor; so too are alcohol and certain antidepressants

What is the gold standard treatment for OSA?

  • Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) — mask at night blows air creating a pneumatic splint keeping airway open.

Describe the efficacy of CPAP.

  • Very effective — improvements in sleep, daytime sleepiness, mood and cardiovascular outcomes.
  • Compliance generally very low — often need behaviour change techniques to encourage adherence.

Describe three lifestyle factors that will increase/reduce severity of OSA.

  • Losing weight
  • Quitting smoking
  • Reducing alcohol

Prompt says “increase severity” but listed answers are protective behaviours that reduce severity — as in the source PDF.

Describe three of the diagnostic criteria for insomnia.

  • Difficulty initiating sleep
  • Difficulty maintaining sleep
  • Waking up earlier than desired
  • Resistance to going to bed
  • Difficulty sleeping without parent/caregiver
  • Daytime difficulties because of sleep
  • Present for 3 months = chronic; 1–3 months = short-term
  • Diagnosed when difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep ≥3 nights/week with daytime difficulties
  • Middle-aged adults: sleep onset >30 min or awake >30 min during desired sleep period clinically significant

Describe the relationship between insomnia and stress levels.

  • May represent a continuous elevated stress response even once external stressor resolved.
  • Elevated stress response across 24-hour day — hyperarousal theory of insomnia.

Describe treatments available for insomnia.

  • First line: Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT).
  • NOTE: Psychotropics not advised — side effects often worse than insomnia; dependence; short-lived effects.

Describe components of CBT-I.

  • Targets unhelpful behaviours, thoughts and beliefs around sleep.
  • Components: Sleep Hygiene; Relaxation Training; Paradoxical Intention (intentionally engage in feared behaviour); Cognitive Therapy; Sleep Restriction Therapy

What is Sleep Restriction Therapy?

Retraining people on when to sleep; often incorporates a certain level of sleep deprivation over the short term.

How does use of hypnotics affect older patients?

  • Consistently associated with higher rates of falls → significant morbidity, mortality, healthcare/social care costs.
  • In overdose, more susceptible to cardiorespiratory depression.
  • Addictive — addiction may be less well recognised than in younger adults.
  • Long-term: reduced social functioning (memory/gait); impaired cognitive function wrongly diagnosed as dementia; mood disturbance/depression/emotional blunting/interdose anxiety.

Social isolation, loneliness and social prescribing

What is the difference between social isolation and loneliness?

  • Social isolation: objective measure of number of contacts — quantity, not quality.
  • Loneliness: subjective feeling about the gap between desired and actual social contact — perceived quality of relationships.

Why is it important to distinguish between them?

  • Possible to have a large social network and still feel lonely.
  • Not all relationships beneficial — abusive relationships can harm health; consider quality as well as quantity.

Describe how loneliness is measured.

Gold standard includes both direct (Community Life Survey single-item) and indirect (UCLA 3-item scale) measures.

Describe how social isolation is measured.

Tools such as the Lubben Social Network scale.

What is social prescribing?

  • Connects people to activities, groups and services in their community to meet practical, social and emotional needs affecting health and wellbeing.
  • Enables GPs, nurses and other primary care professionals to refer to local non-clinical services.
  • NOTE: Biggest determinants of wellbeing are social — if root cause is social rather than medical, best solution may be social.

Describe how social prescribing works.

  • Referral to a link worker (community navigator / wellbeing connector / social prescriber).
  • Link workers give people time, focus on ‘what matters to me?’, co-produce a simple personalised care and support plan, support people to take control of health/wellbeing.
  • Also support existing community groups to be accessible/sustainable and help people start new groups.
  • Services vary significantly in scope and sophistication depending on local support and funding.

Describe who is suitable for social prescribing.
